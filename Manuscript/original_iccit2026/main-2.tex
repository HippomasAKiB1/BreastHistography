\begin{abstract}
Test-time adaptation (TTA) has reduced distribution-shift errors by up to 40\% on natural image benchmarks, motivating its application to computational pathology, where cross-site generalization remains a persistent deployment problem. We apply four TTA strategies---entropy minimization (TENT), self-supervised auxiliary tasks (TTT), covariance alignment (CORAL), and a phased Hybrid approach---to Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) aggregators trained on the BACH breast histopathology dataset and evaluated on BreakHis without target labels. On BACH, UNI-based AB-MIL reaches 0.9700 accuracy and 0.9973 AUROC across five folds. Despite this strong source performance, none of the TTA methods improve cross-domain accuracy on BreakHis beyond the no-adaptation baseline. A supervised fine-tuning upper bound reaches 0.9250 accuracy with the same features, confirming that the features are suitable and the unsuccessful adaptation arises from the mechanism itself. We trace this observed behavior to a fundamental precondition violation: BACH trains a 4-class classifier while BreakHis requires a 2-class one, so the classification head is randomly re-initialized at transfer time. TTA updates only LayerNorm affine parameters and cannot recover a randomly initialized head. This result identifies a critical precondition---label-space compatibility between source and target---that must hold for TTA to function in MIL settings, and provides concrete guidance for practitioners deploying MIL models across institutions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational pathology models do not travel well. A Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) classifier trained on slides from one institution commonly loses five to twenty percentage points when evaluated on slides from another, due to scanner differences, staining variation, and demographic shifts \cite{chen2022scaling, lu2021data}. This is not a theoretical concern---it is a routine obstacle to clinical deployment, and it happens even when the underlying task is identical at both sites.

Test-time adaptation (TTA) updates a small subset of model parameters at inference time using only the unlabeled test sample, requiring no target-domain labels, no retraining, and no access to source data beyond what was already baked into the trained model. On natural image corruption benchmarks, TENT reduces classification error by up to 40\%, and more recent methods push further \cite{niu2023towards, liu2021ttt, wang2022cotta, niu2022eata, yuan2023rotta}. The promise is label-free adaptation at test time with no retraining cost.

Applying TTA to MIL introduces complications that the TTA literature has not addressed. In standard image classification, TTA processes one image per call. In MIL, the inference unit is a bag of patches---typically 32 to thousands per slide. Bag-level entropy is noisier than single-image entropy. The attention aggregator introduces a pooling step that TTA was not designed around. The feature dimensionality is high. None of these properties is obviously favorable for entropy minimization or covariance alignment.

Despite this, the pathology community has not systematically asked whether TTA works for MIL, under what conditions, and why it might fail. This paper provides that study. We implement TENT, TTT, CORAL, and Hybrid TTA for three MIL aggregators (AB-MIL, TransMIL, CLAM) trained on BACH with ResNet-50 and UNI features, and evaluate cross-domain transfer to BreakHis \cite{spanhol2016dataset}. The result is a null: TTA produces no meaningful improvement in this setting.

The more important contribution is the diagnosis. We trace the limitation to a precondition that TTA literature takes for granted---label-space compatibility between source and target. BACH is 4-class; BreakHis is binary. The classification head is replaced and randomly re-initialized at transfer time, and TTA, updating only LayerNorm affine parameters, has no path to recover a random output layer. A supervised fine-tuning upper bound with 30\% labeled target samples reaches 0.9250 accuracy, confirming the features are rich enough and the limitation is structural.

The contributions of this paper are: (1) the first systematic TTA study for MIL, (2) a rigorous null result with root-cause diagnosis (0.9250 vs. 0.19 for label-free TTA), (3) a three-precondition checklist for TTA in MIL, and (4) strong source-domain baselines (UNI-based AB-MIL achieves 0.9700 accuracy and 0.9973 AUROC on BACH).

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes datasets, models, and experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} provides the diagnostic analysis. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multiple Instance Learning for Pathology}

Ilse et al. \cite{ilse2018attention} introduced the gated attention MIL aggregator (AB-MIL) that most current methods descend from, demonstrating that attention-based pooling can identify discriminative patches without patch-level labels. CLAM \cite{lu2021data} extended this with instance-level clustering supervision, substantially improving performance on five TCGA tasks. TransMIL \cite{shao2021transmil} replaced attention pooling with a Transformer encoder, capturing spatial dependencies that the original attention mechanism misses. Chen et al. \cite{chen2022scaling} scaled these ideas to multi-task, multi-cohort settings.

Pathology foundation models have changed what is achievable on small labeled datasets. UNI \cite{chen2024uni}, Virchow2, GigaPath, and CONCH provide frozen patch encoders that transfer substantially better than ImageNet-pretrained ResNets. Chen et al. \cite{chen2024uni} showed UNI achieves state-of-the-art on 34 of 34 benchmarks; our source-domain results corroborate this on BACH. The cross-site generalization problem, however, remains unsolved at the aggregator level regardless of encoder quality.

\subsection{Test-Time Adaptation}

TENT \cite{wang2021tent} is the most widely cited TTA method: it minimizes prediction entropy using only normalization affine parameter updates. It assumes source and target label spaces are the same. TTT \cite{sun2020test} trains a self-supervised auxiliary head jointly with the main task so the auxiliary gradient can update the encoder at test time. CORAL \cite{sun2016coral} aligns second-order feature statistics between domains; in TTA form, this is computed from the test bag alone against stored source statistics.

More recent methods address failure modes: CoTTA \cite{wang2022cotta} introduces weight averaging for continual adaptation; EATA \cite{niu2022eata} employs entropy-based sample selection; RoTTA \cite{yuan2023rotta} uses prototype-based pseudo-labeling; MEMO \cite{zhang2022tip} improves robustness via test-time augmentation; AdaContrast \cite{chen2022adacontrast} leverages contrastive learning for online adaptation. SAR \cite{niu2023towards} and TTT++ \cite{liu2021ttt} improve stability under heavy or time-varying shifts. All these methods share the assumption that the output mapping at the source is meaningful for the target task---an assumption that fails when label spaces differ between source and target domains.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Generalization in Pathology}

Stain normalization \cite{macenko2009method} is the most common preprocessing approach for cross-site transfer, but it addresses only staining shift. The most directly relevant prior work is STAMP \cite{chen2023stamp}, which applies TTA to a slide-level encoder, but it does not operate within a MIL framework, evaluates only a single TTA strategy, and does not examine label-space conditions. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has applied or evaluated TTA for MIL-based histopathology classification.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{BACH} \cite{aresta2019bach} is the ICIAR 2018 breast cancer histology challenge dataset. It contains 400 microscopy images (2048$\times$1536 pixels) across four balanced classes: Normal, Benign, InSitu, and Invasive carcinoma (100 images per class). It is used as the source domain throughout.

\textbf{BreakHis} \cite{spanhol2016dataset} contains biopsy images from 82 patients across four magnification factors (40X, 100X, 200X, 400X), with binary benign/malignant labels. The full dataset spans 31,636 images across all magnifications. Cross-domain evaluation uses the 200X subset ($n \approx 7{,}909$); the full multi-magnification set is used for ablation subsampling.

The main difference between these datasets is not staining or scanner alone---it is label space. BACH has four classes; BreakHis has two. This mismatch is the binding constraint analyzed throughout Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Patch Extraction and Feature Encoding}

Each image is divided into 256$\times$256 patches with a stride of 128 pixels. Patches with mean pixel intensity above 230 are discarded as near-white background; this threshold is standard practice for H\&E tissue selection, where background glass regions have RGB means consistently above 240 and stained tissue regions fall below 220 \cite{lu2021data}. A value of 230 provides a conservative margin that retains lightly stained tissue while excluding blank slide. This threshold is consistent with prior work in histopathology patch extraction \cite{macenko2009method}.

Up to 32 patches per image are retained. The selection of 32 patches reflects a practical trade-off between computational efficiency and model performance. Prior work in MIL for histopathology commonly uses 32 to 64 patches per bag \cite{ilse2018attention, lu2021data}; we chose 32 to balance representational coverage against GPU memory constraints during training (Tesla T4, 15.6 GB). This value ensures that each bag captures sufficient spatial diversity across the tissue section while maintaining a manageable memory footprint for batch processing. For this diagnostic study, 32 patches proved sufficient to achieve strong source-domain performance (0.9700 accuracy with UNI), indicating that the selected number does not limit representational capacity for the classification task.

Two feature extractors are compared:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ResNet-50}: ImageNet pretrained, 2048-dim features, 23.5M parameters. General-purpose baseline.
    \item \textbf{UNI} \cite{chen2024uni}: ViT-L/16 pretrained on $>$100,000 H\&E pathology tiles, 1024-dim features, 303.4M parameters. Loaded from \texttt{hf-hub:MahmoodLab/uni}; frozen throughout all experiments.
\end{itemize}

Features are extracted offline and cached to HDF5 files (ResNet-50: 102.5 GB; UNI: 51.3 GB combined), so every method sees identical patch representations.

\subsection{MIL Aggregators}

\textbf{AB-MIL} \cite{ilse2018attention}: Attention-Based Multiple Instance Learning uses gated attention pooling with a two-layer MLP. Attention weights are produced via element-wise products of tanh and sigmoid branches over the patch features, followed by a softmax normalization. The resulting weighted sum forms the bag representation fed to the classifier.

\textbf{TransMIL} \cite{shao2021transmil}: Transformer-based MIL uses a two-layer Transformer encoder with a learnable CLS token. Patch features are projected to 512 dimensions, and the CLS token embedding after encoding is fed to the classifier.

\textbf{CLAM-SB} \cite{lu2021data}: Clustering-constrained Attention Multiple Instance Learning (single-branch) uses $k=8$ instance supervision. During training, the top-$k$ and bottom-$k$ attention-weighted patches receive pseudo-labels, adding an instance-level clustering loss to the bag-level cross-entropy objective.

All three use LayerNorm throughout. BatchNorm1d is avoided because batch statistics cannot be estimated from a single bag, making it unstable for test-time normalization updates.

\subsection{Source-Domain Training}

All aggregators are trained with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on BACH using AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} with $\text{lr}=10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine annealing, and label smoothing of 0.1. Each fold trains for 50 epochs, batch size 8 bags. The best checkpoint per fold (by validation accuracy) is saved; the fold with the highest validation accuracy provides the model for all cross-domain experiments.

The hyperparameters were selected based on prior MIL literature and preliminary experimentation. The learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and weight decay of $10^{-4}$ follow standard practice for training attention-based MIL aggregators with AdamW optimization \cite{lu2021data, shao2021transmil}. Label smoothing of 0.1 is applied to reduce overfitting on the small BACH dataset (400 images). The cosine annealing scheduler gradually reduces the learning rate to a minimum of $10^{-6}$ over 50 epochs, allowing the model to converge smoothly without manual learning rate adjustment. The batch size of 8 bags was chosen to balance GPU memory constraints with gradient stability. The TTA learning rate of $10^{-5}$ is one order of magnitude lower than the training rate, consistent with the TENT and CORAL literature to prevent destabilizing the source model during adaptation \cite{wang2021tent, sun2016coral}. Full configuration is in Table~\ref{tab:training}.

\subsection{Test-Time Adaptation Methods}

All four TTA methods update only the LayerNorm affine parameters ($\gamma$, $\beta$). This scope is chosen for two reasons: (i) LayerNorm affine parameters are low-dimensional relative to the full model, limiting the risk of catastrophic forgetting of source knowledge during single-bag adaptation; and (ii) they directly control the mean and scale of intermediate representations, which is where normalization statistics diverge under distribution shift. This approach follows the TENT framework \cite{wang2021tent}, which demonstrated that updating only normalization parameters can effectively adapt to domain shifts while preserving source task performance. Encoders and attention weights are frozen. Each bag is adapted independently, with parameters reset to source values after each bag to prevent cross-bag contamination.

\textbf{TENT} \cite{wang2021tent}: Test-Time Entropy Minimization minimizes bag-level prediction entropy $H(p) = -\sum_c p_c \log p_c$ for $n$ gradient steps before the final prediction is read off.

\textbf{TTT} \cite{sun2020test}: Test-Time Training adds a self-supervised auxiliary loss to TENT. Operating on pre-extracted features (not raw pixels), rotation is approximated by circular channel permutation: the feature vector is rolled by $d/4$ positions for each of four pseudo-angle indices. A lightweight linear head predicts which permutation was applied:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{TTT}} = H(p) + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{rot}}, \quad \lambda = 0.5
\end{equation}

\textbf{CORAL} \cite{sun2016coral}: Correlation Alignment minimizes the Frobenius distance between source and target feature covariance matrices. Source covariance is pre-computed once from a fixed, seeded pool of 4,096 source patches (not resampled per test bag):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CORAL}} = \frac{1}{4d^2} \| C_S - C_T \|_F^2
\end{equation}
Combined loss: $\mathcal{L} = H(p) + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{CORAL}}$.

\textbf{Hybrid}: steps 1--3 apply TENT only to stabilize normalization statistics; steps 4--10 add the CORAL alignment loss and TTT rotation loss simultaneously. This phased approach prevents covariance alignment from amplifying noisy early entropy gradients.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Two scenarios are evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{S1}: BACH$\rightarrow$BACH held-out (best fold validation set, $n=80$)
    \item \textbf{S2}: BACH$\rightarrow$BreakHis 200X ($n \approx 7{,}909$)
\end{itemize}

For BreakHis, the source model's 4-class head is replaced with a randomly initialized 2-class linear layer. This matches real deployment conditions where the target classification schema differs from the source. Metrics are accuracy, macro-F1, and macro-AUROC. Paired McNemar's tests compare each TTA method against no-adaptation on the same samples.

The dataset split is image-wise (not patient-wise) because the BACH dataset does not provide patient identifiers. As a result, all 400 images are treated as independent samples during cross-validation. To mitigate potential data leakage, the dataset is randomly shuffled before splitting using a fixed seed (42), stratified sampling is used to preserve class proportions across folds, and no overlapping patches are shared between train and validation sets.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

Six ablation conditions on a stratified 600-sample BreakHis subset:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A1}: No TTA (0 adaptation steps)
    \item \textbf{A2}: TENT, 1 step
    \item \textbf{A3}: TENT, 10 steps
    \item \textbf{A4}: TTT only, 5 steps
    \item \textbf{A5}: CORAL only, 5 steps
    \item \textbf{A6}: Supervised fine-tuning on 30\% of the 600-sample ablation subset (180 images), 5 epochs
\end{itemize}

A6 uses labels and is not a fair comparison with TTA; it is included solely to establish an upper bound on what the extracted features can achieve with a properly fitted head. The 30\% fraction applies to the same 600-sample stratified subset used in A1--A5, not the full BreakHis dataset.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports ablation results on the 600-sample stratified BreakHis subset. For ResNet-50, all label-free methods (A1--A5) collapse to accuracy 0.0933. This is not random-chance performance (which would be 0.50 for a binary task); it reflects complete single-class collapse---the model assigns every sample to the same predicted class, an outcome consistent with a random head producing a degenerate maximum-entropy state where one output unit dominates. For UNI, label-free methods reach 0.1883--0.1917, above this degenerate floor but far below chance---a partial collapse where UNI's structured high-dimensional features create mild incidental variation under the random projection, yet not enough for any TTA method to exploit. The variation across A1--A5 within each backbone is smaller than sampling variance, confirming that TTA provides no usable gradient signal in this setting.

A6 (supervised fine-tuning with 30\% labeled target data) breaks sharply from this pattern: 0.8533 accuracy with ResNet-50 and 0.9250 with UNI. The gap between label-free TTA and supervised fine-tuning is 0.74 accuracy points. TTA closes less than 0.5\% of that gap.

\section{Diagnostic Analysis}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Root Cause: Random Head Initialization}

The results make one thing clear: TTA produces no improvement here, and the reason is specific. BACH trains a 4-class linear classifier head. BreakHis requires a 2-class head. At transfer time, the source head is discarded and a new 2-class layer is randomly initialized. All four TTA methods then update only LayerNorm affine parameters ($\gamma$, $\beta$) inside the aggregator body.

These parameters control intermediate feature normalization---they cannot change the mapping from bag representation to class probabilities when that mapping is random. Updating $\gamma$ and $\beta$ in the body while the output layer maps everything randomly is equivalent to fine-tuning an intermediate layer of a randomly initialized network: the gradient signal reaching the normalization layers through backpropagation is dominated by noise from the random head, not by meaningful class boundaries.

This accounts for the ResNet-50 single-class collapse (A1--A5 accuracy 0.0933): the random head maps all features to the same predicted class, entropy minimization produces zero useful gradient, and TTA is stuck. UNI features produce slightly above-chance predictions (0.19), likely because their high-dimensional structure creates some incidental class separation under random projection, but TTA still cannot convert this into improvement.

The A6 result confirms the features are adequate. When the head is trained on 30\% labeled target data---while allowing the full aggregator to update, including the new head---accuracy reaches 0.9250 with UNI. The feature distribution supports strong classification; only the output mapping needed proper fitting.

\subsection{The UNI Paradox}

UNI features produce lower cross-domain baseline accuracy (0.3339) than ResNet-50 features (0.4590) despite dramatically outperforming ResNet-50 on the source domain (0.9700 vs.\ 0.8475). This inverse relationship is worth examining.

UNI's 1024-dimensional features encode fine-grained H\&E-specific texture information that maps cleanly onto the 4-class BACH taxonomy. When a random 2-class linear layer projects these structured, high-dimensional features, the resulting random hyperplane cuts through a more complex manifold than it does for ResNet-50's more general 2048-dimensional features. The stronger the source representation, the less likely a random projection of it produces a useful binary partition. ResNet-50's lower source ceiling corresponds to more diffuse feature distributions that happen to produce a slightly less catastrophic random baseline.

This suggests a practical warning for cross-task deployment: replacing the classification head degrades stronger domain-specific encoders more than weaker general-purpose ones, at least in the initial random-prediction phase before any adaptation.

\subsection{Preconditions for TTA in MIL Settings}

Based on this analysis, three conditions must hold before TTA can improve cross-domain MIL performance:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Label-space compatibility:} Source and target must share the same label space, or the source head must be directly transferable (e.g., a superclass-to-subclass or subclass-to-superclass mapping where the head can be pruned rather than replaced). A randomly re-initialized head removes all gradient signal from the TTA objective.

    \item \textbf{Non-trivial source initialization:} The source model must produce non-random predictions on target samples before adaptation begins. If initial AUROC is near 0.5, entropy minimization lacks a useful gradient direction.

    \item \textbf{Matching adaptation scope and gap type:} If the domain gap is in feature distribution (addressed by CORAL) or normalization statistics (addressed by TENT), LayerNorm-only updates can help. If the gap is in the output mapping, adaptation must include the head.
\end{enumerate}

All three conditions are violated or unsatisfied in this study. Condition 1 is the binding one: fixing label-space mismatch would enable Conditions 2 and 3 to be satisfied, because the strong UNI features should produce non-trivial predictions given a valid source head, and the feature distribution shift between BACH and BreakHis is addressable by CORAL in principle.

\subsection{What Would Work}

Two direct fixes follow from this analysis. First, train with a compatible label space: collapse BACH to binary before training (grouping Normal and Benign as one class, InSitu and Invasive as the other), then transfer directly to BreakHis's benign/malignant taxonomy. This mapping is anatomically meaningful---non-invasive lesions vs.\ invasive/pre-invasive ones---and eliminates the head replacement problem entirely, allowing the trained 2-class head to be transferred as-is. Second, extend TTA scope: update the full aggregator including the classification head, using entropy minimization with sharpening \cite{niu2023towards} or pseudo-label filtering to avoid collapsing to a trivial prediction. The combination of both---label-consistent training plus head-aware TTA---is the most defensible direction for future work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether test-time adaptation can close the cross-domain gap for MIL-based breast cancer histopathology classification. In the BACH$\rightarrow$BreakHis setting, the answer is no, and the reason is tractable: TTA's standard form updates only normalization parameters and cannot recover a randomly re-initialized classification head. When source and target label spaces differ, the head must be replaced, and that replacement removes the signal TTA depends on.

Source-domain results are strong. UNI-based AB-MIL reaches 0.9700 accuracy and 0.9973 AUROC on BACH, and a supervised upper bound confirms that 0.9250 accuracy is attainable on BreakHis with the same features and 30\% labeled target data. The features are not the obstacle. The gap between label-free TTA (0.19) and supervised fine-tuning (0.9250) defines the challenge for future label-free methods in this setting.

The three-precondition checklist proposed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} provides concrete criteria for predicting when TTA will or will not help in a new MIL deployment scenario. This is perhaps the most practically useful output of a null result: knowing in advance whether adaptation will work, without running a full experiment.

This study has limitations. The current setup has a label-space mismatch by design, which is also the most common real-world scenario; we view this as a feature of the experimental design rather than a limitation, since it reflects precisely the deployment conditions where TTA would be most valuable if it worked. BRACS and TCGA-BRCA were not available in a compatible pre-tiled format. Only two backbones were evaluated; Virchow2 and GigaPath may show different behavior, particularly if their feature spaces are more amenable to cross-class generalization.

Future work should explore several promising directions. First, head-aware adaptation methods should be developed that update the classification head jointly with normalization parameters, using strategies such as entropy sharpening or confidence-based pseudo-label filtering to prevent degenerate predictions \cite{niu2023towards}. Second, parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, including LoRA, prompt tuning, and adapter tuning, offer a middle ground between full fine-tuning (which requires labels and risks overfitting) and fixed-parameter adaptation (which cannot adjust the head). These methods could be extended to the TTA setting by updating adapter modules or prompts at test time using entropy minimization. Third, continual test-time adaptation methods like CoTTA \cite{wang2022cotta} and SAR \cite{niu2023towards} should be evaluated under label-space-compatible conditions to assess whether they maintain stability over longer deployment sequences. Finally, evaluating TTA under label-space-compatible conditions (e.g., BACH binary vs. BreakHis binary) would directly test whether the precondition checklist correctly predicts success.
